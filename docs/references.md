# References

## Papers

McCulloch, W. and Pitts, W. (1943). A logical calculus of the ideas immanent
in nervous activity. The threshold unit that gave the field its vocabulary.

Rosenblatt, F. (1958). The perceptron: a probabilistic model for information
storage and organization in the brain. The learning rule in chapter 02.

Minsky, M. and Papert, S. (1969). Perceptrons. The XOR argument.

Rumelhart, D., Hinton, G. and Williams, R. (1986). Learning representations
by back-propagating errors. Chapter 06 in its original form.

LeCun, Y., Bottou, L., Bengio, Y. and Haffner (1998). Gradient-based learning
applied to document recognition. Where MNIST comes from.

Glorot, X. and Bengio, Y. (2010). Understanding the difficulty of training
deep feedforward neural networks. Xavier initialization.

Srivastava, N. et al. (2014). Dropout: a simple way to prevent neural
networks from overfitting.

Kingma, D. and Ba, J. (2014). Adam: a method for stochastic optimization.

He, K., Zhang, X., Ren, S. and Sun, J. (2015). Delving deep into rectifiers.
He initialization.

Ioffe, S. and Szegedy, C. (2015). Batch normalization.

He, K. et al. (2015). Deep residual learning for image recognition.

## Books

Goodfellow, I., Bengio, Y. and Courville, A. Deep Learning. MIT Press, 2016.
Free online. Chapters 6 to 8 cover this guide at greater length and depth.

Nielsen, M. Neural Networks and Deep Learning. Free online. The clearest
introduction to backpropagation available, and it builds an MNIST classifier
from scratch as well.

Bishop, C. Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning. Springer, 2006. The
statistical framing behind the maximum likelihood argument in chapter 05.

## Courses

CS231n, Stanford. The notes on optimization and backpropagation are worth
reading even though the course is about convolutional networks.

Fast.ai, Practical Deep Learning for Coders. The opposite order: results
first, theory later.

## Documentation

PyTorch tutorials, at pytorch.org. Start with the 60 minute blitz, then the
autograd tutorial.

NumPy broadcasting rules, at numpy.org. Half of shape debugging is
broadcasting.
